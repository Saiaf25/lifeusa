2. Audience And Reader Need

Primary audience

U.S. donors who own shares in a brokerage account and are considering a charitable gift, especially donors with appreciated publicly traded stock.

Reader stage

Middle of funnel. The reader already wants to give and is comparing the safest, most suitable way to transfer value.

Reader problem

“I own shares that may have gained value, but I do not know whether the charity accepts them, how the transfer works, what happens to the gain, or which records I need.”

Reason to leave

The page delays the answer, reads like tax promotion, skips the transfer mechanics, or implies a guaranteed result without explaining the conditions.

Questions the page must answer early

  • Can I donate stock directly to a charity?
  • How is an in-kind transfer different from selling shares and donating cash?
  • How do I begin the transfer with my brokerage and the charity?
  • Which records should I keep, and when might Form 8283 apply?
  • Does the tax treatment apply automatically? It does not, and the page must say so.

5. Format Decision

Selected Format

A focused how-to guide, not a general benefits listicle and not an interactive tax product. Use one definition, one fit table, a six-step numbered process, one direct-versus-cash comparison, a records checklist, and six concise FAQs.

Article Thesis

Donating appreciated stock directly to a qualified charity can be a useful giving option for some donors. The transfer differs from selling shares and donating the proceeds, and the tax result depends on the asset, holding period, recipient, documentation, and current rules.

Searcher Promise

7. Intro Guidance

Why here: The searcher needs an immediate answer before tax detail or organizational promotion.

Reader stage: Deciding whether the method is relevant.

Keyword ownership: donate stock, stock donations to charity, charitable stock donation.

What Angela should write:

  • Answer yes in the first sentence: many qualified charities can receive stock through an in-kind transfer, but the donor must confirm acceptance and current instructions.
  • Explain in one sentence that transferring shares is different from selling them and donating cash.
  • Say that appreciated publicly traded stock may receive different treatment from cash or loss shares, subject to the donor's facts and current rules.
  • Promise the practical route: compare, transfer, confirm receipt, preserve records, and ask a qualified professional about the tax result.

Transition out: “The first decision is not how many shares to give. It is whether a direct transfer fits the asset and the gift you are considering.”

What to avoid: A stock-market primer, a market forecast, “giving hack,” “golden ticket,” “stop paying taxes,” or a donation CTA before the reader understands the method.

Suggested opening shape: Direct answer → one-sentence distinction → conditional benefit → page promise. Keep it to roughly 110 to 150 words.

8. Detailed Article Outline For Angela

Eight core H2 sections, supported by specific H3s. Every section below states its purpose, reader stage, keyword owner, required content, transition, and exclusions.

H2: What Does It Mean to Donate Stock?

Why here: Define the method before discussing benefits or tax treatment.

Reader stage: Building a mental model.

Keyword ownership: charitable contribution of stock, donating securities to charity, non cash charitable contributions.

H3: An in-kind transfer, in plain language

  • Explain that shares move from the donor's brokerage account to the qualified charity or DAF without the donor selling first.
  • Clarify that the recipient becomes the owner and the donor cannot reclaim the shares.

H3: Which securities may be accepted?

  • Center publicly traded stock. Mention mutual funds and ETFs only when the charity and brokerage can process them.
  • Keep private, restricted, or illiquid securities in a short scope note because they require additional acceptance and valuation review.

Internal link: Link “why people use different giving methods” to Why Donate to Charity?

Transition out: “Once the transfer itself is clear, the next question is whether the asset is a good candidate for this kind of gift.”

What to avoid: Lessons on trading hours, dividends, portfolio allocation, or market terminology that does not help complete the gift.

H2: When Donating Appreciated Stock May Make Sense

Why here: Help the reader identify the case this strategy is designed for without giving personal investment advice.

Reader stage: Comparing eligibility and fit.

Keyword ownership: charitable contribution of appreciated stock, appreciated stock, gifting appreciated stock to charity.

H3: Appreciated publicly traded shares

  • Define appreciated stock as shares worth more now than the donor paid.
  • Explain that property type, holding period, recipient, valuation, income limits, and current law all matter.

H3: Shares that have lost value

  • Explain that a sale-and-cash-gift route may deserve comparison.
  • Do not select the route for the reader; tell them to compare outcomes with a qualified advisor.

H3: Private, restricted, or hard-to-transfer shares

  • State that acceptance, transfer, and valuation can require additional review.
  • Tell the reader to contact the charity before starting any paperwork.

Required fit table:

SituationWhat to exploreSafe wording
Shares increased in valueDirect transfer versus selling first“A direct transfer may change the capital-gain and deduction analysis.”
Shares lost valueSale and cash gift may need comparison“Ask a tax professional before choosing a route.”

Transition out: “If the asset appears to fit, confirm that the recipient can accept it and request the exact transfer instructions.”

What to avoid: “Always better than cash,” market-peak language, income-based promises, or a universal recommendation based only on holding period.

H2: How to Donate Stock to a Charity: A Six-Step Checklist

Why here: This is the primary search answer and the page's largest information-gain section.

Reader stage: Ready to act but needs process confidence.

Keyword ownership: how to donate stock to charity, stock donation to nonprofit, charities that accept stock donations.

H3: 1. Confirm the charity and the asset

  • Check that the organization qualifies and accepts the specific security.
  • Link the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search.

H3: 2. Request the charity's current transfer instructions

  • Ask for the legal entity name, custodian, account information, DTC number if applicable, and donor-notification process.
  • Warn that instructions can change and should never be copied from an old post.

H3: 3. Ask the brokerage how to initiate an in-kind transfer

  • Use the broker's current form or secure workflow.
  • Confirm ticker, share count, registration, requested date, signatures, and any special requirements.

H3: 4. Coordinate timing without trying to call the market

  • Start early enough for both organizations to process the request.
  • Do not equate clicking submit with the legal contribution date; verify the applicable rule and transfer record.

H3: 5. Notify the charity and confirm receipt

  • Tell the charity what security and share count to expect, using its requested notification route.
  • Obtain a written acknowledgment and keep brokerage evidence that the transfer completed.

H3: 6. Give the records to a tax professional

  • Ask which forms, valuation support, and acknowledgments apply to the specific gift.
  • Link official Form 8283 instructions as a resource, not a substitute for advice.

Transition out: “The process explains how shares move. The next comparison explains why a donor might transfer them directly instead of selling first.”

What to avoid: “Instant,” “automatic,” or any LIFE brokerage coordinate that finance has not reconfirmed on the publication date.

H2: How the Stock Donation Tax Deduction Works at a High Level

Why here: Answer the tax question while preserving the canonical tax guide's broader ownership.

Reader stage: Checking whether the possible benefit applies.

Keyword ownership: charitable contribution of stock, stock donation tax deduction, donated stock deduction.

H3: The organization and contribution must qualify

  • State that a deduction is conditional on the organization, asset, documentation, and current law.
  • Explain that gifts to individuals do not qualify as charitable-contribution deductions.

H3: Valuation depends on the property and facts

  • Explain fair market value only at a high level.
  • Link IRS Publication 561 for current valuation rules and distinguish readily traded stock from private securities.

H3: A deduction is not a refund

  • Say plainly that a deduction reduces taxable income rather than returning the gift amount dollar for dollar.
  • Note that itemizing status, income limits, and current-year rules can change the result.

Internal link: Link to the general charitable donation tax guide only after its stale material is refreshed. Until then, use the IRS sources.

Transition out: “The tax explanation is only useful if the donor can support the gift with complete records.”

What to avoid: “Full market value” as an unconditional promise, personalized calculations, copied annual thresholds, or a claim that giving produces a profit.

YMYL review gate: Verify all tax language against the current IRS material on the drafting date and have a qualified reviewer check the final section before publication.

H2: What Records Should You Keep for a Stock Donation?

Why here: Documentation turns an attractive idea into a defensible gift record.

Reader stage: Preparing to preserve evidence and file accurately.

Keyword ownership: form 8283 noncash charitable contributions, stock donation form, non cash charitable contributions.

H3: Transfer and receipt records

  • Charity legal name, qualification check, brokerage request, transfer confirmation, dates, ticker, share count, and acknowledgment.
  • Keep both the date shares left the donor's account and the date received when records differ.

H3: Valuation, basis, and holding-period records

  • Preserve relevant statements, value support, acquisition information, and professional notes.
  • Do not imply that the charity calculates or certifies the donor's deduction.

H3: When Form 8283 may apply

  • Use the current IRS Instructions for Form 8283. The December 2025 instructions say the form reports noncash contributions and generally applies when the deduction for a noncash contribution exceeds $500.
  • State that section, appraisal, and filing requirements depend on the asset and amount. Publicly traded securities have specific treatment.

Transition out: “Good records support a suitable gift, but they do not make every security or every situation a good fit.”

What to avoid: Treating a receipt as a valuation, presenting the form threshold without a source date, or giving form-completion advice.

H2: When a Stock Donation May Not Be the Right Fit

Why here: A balanced boundary builds trust and prevents the article from reading like a tax pitch.

Reader stage: Stress-testing the decision.

Keyword ownership: No forced exact-match keyword. This section supports trust and long-tail decision intent.

H3: Acceptance or timing problems

  • The charity cannot accept the security, the brokerage process is unclear, or the transfer may miss the intended contribution date.

H3: Loss shares, cash needs, or complex assets

  • The shares have lost value, the donor needs the asset for living or planning needs, or the security is private, restricted, or difficult to value.

H3: Direct gift or donor-advised fund?

  • A direct gift sends the asset to the selected charity. A DAF may support later grant recommendations but brings its own fees, timing, and administrative questions.
  • Keep this comparison to one short subsection and link a future dedicated guide when available.

Transition out: “If a direct gift still fits after these checks, the final step is to obtain LIFE's current instructions.”

What to avoid: Telling the reader which security to sell, retain, or donate, or expanding DAFs into a second article.

H2: How to Donate Stock to LIFE

Why here: Convert practical intent only after the reader understands the method and its boundaries.

Reader stage: Ready to contact the organization.

Keyword ownership: Brand-plus-method phrases only. Do not stuff the primary keyword again.

H3: Request the current transfer instructions

  • Link to LIFE's ways to give page.
  • Publish a dedicated contact route only after finance confirms it.

H3: Tell LIFE how to identify the gift

  • After finance confirms the workflow, list the donor-notification fields LIFE actually needs to match the incoming shares to the donor and designation.
  • Remind the reader to confirm completion with both the brokerage and LIFE.

Conclusion transition and CTA: Summarize the method in three ideas: confirm fit, transfer through verified instructions, and preserve evidence. Then use one CTA: “If you are considering a stock gift to LIFE, start with our ways-to-give page and request the current transfer instructions.”

What to avoid: Carrying forward the old account number, DTC number, brokerage name, email, or phone without same-day finance confirmation. Do not add competing donation buttons.
